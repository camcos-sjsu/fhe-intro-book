37  Concluding Remarks and Future Directions

We begin by acknowledging our sponsor, 0xPARC, and supervisors, Dr. Dashiell Fryer and Dr. Yan Zhang. We thank you for your invaluable support, collaboration and mentorship.

Our work can serve as an introductory textbook on mathematical foundations, computational methods and applications of FHE. Readers are exposed to a wide range of operations, algorithms and more, including: function approximations, division, comparisons, statistical computations, sorting, and matrix operations, alongside compelling alternative, discrete computational representations, and some notes on the underlying mechanics of CKKS.

Looking forward, we see a wide range of opportunities for future work in this context. We will begin by discussing where there may be room for concrete mathematical advancement or continued work, and subsequently theorize a bit on general future directions and real-world applications in this field.

37.1 Further Directions: Concrete

Future mathematical work in this domain could explore more efficient methods for computing multiplicative inverse (division), e.g. determining suitable convergence ranges for Newton and Goldschmidt methods as well as investigating potentially faster algorithms. Along with division, another foundational and highly applicable object to refine is our approximation(s) of the sign function– having a sufficient implementation for this would in turn enable many additional operations. In particular, we could investigate whether Fourier transforms of sign-function approximations, such as the hyperbolic tangent, could help us obtain useful approximations of \(1/x\), which could potentially serve as starting points for Newton’s method.

It is also of interest to explore the possibility of simultaneous computation on multiple representations of an element: e.g., storing a value with both its discrete and approximate numerical representation, performing any relevent computations on both, and potentially developing a function that selects whichever representation is more efficient or appropriate for a given operation. Related work could further look into Boolean algebra, in particular its connections to machine arithmetic and modular reduction. There is existing research on such topics which should be examined.

We could also further refine multiple linear regression. And, broadly speaking, it may be of interest to optimize matrix representations and operations within FHE.

37.2 Further Directions: Theoretical

Now continuing with some theoretical directions: we claim the evolving technological landscape, in particular with the nearly societal adoption of artificial intelligence, presents not just ample opportunity but also substantial value for the integration of FHE. As individuals and institutions increasingly use AI to perform tasks on sensitive data, questions on privacy and security become central.

This appears even more relevant with recent shifts from generative to agentic AI usage. In the longer term, perhaps there could be a critical synergy between FHE and agentic AI: AI being leveraged to create, validate and strengthen such FHE systems, then agents being deployed in high-stakes domains to securely process/compute on sensitive data. Such domains could include government, medicine/science/academia, finance/commerce/banking, and personal online presences such as social media or online dating. We’ll brainstorm various specific use cases below, and expand on/flesh out a few.

37.2.1 Government and Public Services

FHE could enable age verification or other forms of identity verification akin to having a sort of national ID: perhaps governments could adopt privacy-preserving digital national ID systems, which is relevant for: voter eligibility verification, access to healthcare and educational institutions, authentication for government/social services, etc. Some nations/regions already operate somewhat like this (Estonia, Singapore, India, European Union), but none necessarily use specifically FHE-based systems.

37.2.2 Medicine, Science and Academia

The field of medicine is ripe with sensitive data, providing many hypothetical use-cases for FHE. For instance, FHE could enable AI-assisted diagnoses, clinical trial matching, genetic research etc. without requiring unencrypted access to patient medical records. A valuable concept is FHE-facilitated collaborative research between hospitals and academic/scientific institutions across different states or even countries, as researchers or institutions could jointly compute on encrypted datasets without directly sharing protected patient information.

These concepts could of course naturally extend to non-medical research of any kind involving sensitive data. Other academic use cases could include transcript verification, evaluating applicant eligibility for admissions, scholarships, graduation etc., or perhaps conducting institution-wide analyses on student demographics, academic outcomes, or any other sensitive statistics which may be used to inform funding and resource allocation decisions, all while keeping relevant data encrypted.

37.2.3 Personal Applications, Online Dating

FHE could find a place not just instituionally/systemically but also in our personal domains, such as job hunting/matching with employers, social media, online dating, etc. There is existing FHE research on these topics, see links if interested: Privacy-Preserving User Profile Matching in Social Networks (2020)1, Hidden Elo: Private Matchmaking through Encrypted Rating Systems (2026)2, Private Verification in Multi-stakeholder Environment and its Application to Stable Matching (2024)3. As a fun final exercise, let’s explore potential intersections between FHE and online dating in more depth.

Online dating is ever on the rise– an avenue once considered unconventional is now incredibly mainstream, with some claiming that online dating is now the leading way for couples to meet.[^stanforddating] This modern dating landscape is accompanied by a unique set of advantages and concerns, particularly with regards to the safety and security of users, both online and off. Perhaps FHE could allow developers and the like to maximize the positive outcomes of online dating while also optimizing for, or entirely ensuring, user safety.

Let’s review a real-life example as motivation: the “Tea” app’s creators had positive intentions– to protect women from some of the risks their specific demographic faces in the dating landscape. Women could anonymously share information, in particular warnings or “red flags”, about men they had dated or interacted with online.

A space meant to protect women turned into the opposite in 2025 when approximately 72,000 user images were compromised, roughly 13,000 of which containing personally identifying media such as selfies, driver’s licenses and passports.[^tea] Some photos even possessed metadata such as coordinates allowing for the tracking of users’ locations. Tea had been storing these sensitive images in an unsecured Google Firebase storage bucket while simultaneously explicitly telling users that their media was deleted immediately upon verification. Soon after this initial breach, a second vulnerability was discovered leading to a leak of about 1.1 million private messages.

Tea faced heavy financial and legal consequences, but that is an incomparable price to the loss of safety users experienced. What if instead of blindly trusting dating platforms with their (highly) sensitive data, users could rely on an FHE-based system that guarantees to process that data while it remains encrypted? Below, let’s brainstorm the design and capabilities of an “FHE dating app” by examining some specific use-cases/desires users may have, and how we may actually implement their FHE solutions.

37.2.3.1 Situation 1: Secret Dealbreakers

Bob only wants to see profiles in his feed that align with his “dealbreakers” he has specified.

Users often provide sensitive data for this purpose, including but not limited to: age, location range/distance, ethnicity, religion, children status, relationship type/dating intentions, substance use, political orientation, education level, etc.

Let’s focus on just location first. Bob only wants to match with users who are located 25 miles or less away, but without revealing his particular location (or theirs).

Working directly with latitude/longitude would require spherical distance formulas with trigonometric functions, which we’d like to avoid. Instead, request user location to be provided as 3D Cartesian coordinates: if the Earth is modelled as approximately a sphere with radius \(R\), a user’s latitude \(\phi\) and longitude \(\lambda\) can be converted to \((x, y, z) = R(\cos\phi\cos\lambda, \cos{\phi}\sin{\lambda}, \sin\phi)\).

Let Alice and Bob’s locations be denoted \(A = (x_A, y_A, z_A)\) and \(B = (x_B, y_B, z_B)\) respectfully. These would be encrypted as \(N/2\)-length ciphertexts \[ \mathsf{ct}_A = \mathsf{enc}(x_A, y_A, z_A, 0, \dots, 0), \mathsf{ct}_B = \mathsf{enc}(x_B, y_B, z_B, 0, \dots, 0) \]

The distance between Alice and Bob would then be \[ D = \sqrt{(x_A-x_B)^2+(y_A-y_B)^2+(z_A-z_B)^2} \] We want to determine if \(D\le25\). To avoid the square root, instead we consider \(D^2\le25^2=625\). We see that computing \(D^2\) in FHE only involves additions, subtractions, and powers (multiplications) which we have natively. The main work is the comparison. For that, we’d want to approximate an indicator function: \[ I(D^2) \approx \begin{cases} 1, & D^2 \leq 625,\\ 0, & D^2 > 625. \end{cases} \]

Let’s alter this slightly. Let \(s = 625-D^2\). Then, the problem reduces to determining whether the encrypted value \(s\) is positive or negative: \[ I(s)= \begin{cases} 1, & s \geq 0,\\ 0, & s < 0. \end{cases} \]

We can approximate the above using a polynomial approximation of a sign or step function.

37.2.3.2 Situation 2: Feeling Shy

Let’s say that instead of her profile being publicly broadcasted to other users, Alice only wants her profile revealed if she has sent a “like” to them first.

Typically, dating app algorithms continuously “decide” which users to place in people’s feeds. But now, Alice will only show up in Bob’s feed if Alice has liked Bob.

Consider \(N/2\)-length ciphertexts \[ \mathsf{ct}_A = \mathsf{enc}(l_{A,1}, l_{A,2}, \dots, l_{A, N/2}), \mathsf{ct}_B = \mathsf{enc}(0, \dots, 0, 1, 0, \dots, 0) \] where \(A\) encodes Alice’s “likes”: \[ l_A,j= \begin{cases} 1, & \text{Alice liked User j},\\ 0, & \text{otherwise}. \end{cases} \] and \(B\) encodes Bob’s identity. Say Bob is the \(j\)-th user, and Alice has liked him. Elementwise multiplying \(AB\) would in that case give \[ \mathsf{ct}_{AB} = \mathsf{enc}(0, \dots, 0, l_{A, j}, 0, \dots, 0) = \mathsf{enc}(0, \dots, 0, 1, 0, \dots, 0); \] using repeated cycles and additions we can from there easily produce \[ \mathsf{ct}_{\Sigma AB} = \mathsf{enc}(l_{A, j}, l_{A, j}, \dots, l_{A, j}) = \mathsf{enc}(1, 1, \dots, 1). \]

Naturally, if Alice had not liked Bob, the result would have been \[ \mathsf{enc}(0, 0, \dots, 0). \]

Now that we possess such a vector, how does that actually translate to Alice’s profile being placed in Bob’s feed? This is where it appears our problem would be best approached within an environment beyond just FHE. Meaning, this has become more of an “access-control, key-management” problem at this point.

37.2.3.3 Situation 3: Offline Connections

Bob had an unfavorable experience dating a coworker, and now he wants to only match with users with no personal connection to his life. On the other hand, Alice is feeling wary of online strangers and wants her profile only revealed to users she does have some personal connection with.

This situation differs a bit from the prior two with regards to the input data required. Here, it would be useful to connect some external source to the dating app, such as Facebook friends or phone contacts, in order to determine personal connections. Obviously, Alice and Bob desire any information provided to be encrypted.

37.3 Wrapping Up

We hope this book serves as both an accessible introduction to FHE and a foundation for a reader’s further exploration. As the demand for privacy-preserving computation continues to grow, we believe FHE has the potential to play an increasingly important role in the future of computing.
